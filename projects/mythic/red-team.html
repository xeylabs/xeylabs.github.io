Mythic under attack.

Since the last entry, Mythic gained key rotation, a Redis store for multi-node, sliding-window pressure counters — and survived five rounds of us attacking it like a real adversary. Twelve findings. Every one fixed, tested, and public. This is the receipts post.

Round 1: the floor was lying

We ran mythicd live and attacked it as a black box. The first finding hurt: a client that solved the proof-of-work natively in milliseconds could simply sleep past the plausibility floor and collect an allow token with zero compute. The floor measured wall-clock time — and the attacker controls the clock.

Worse, the calibration was inverted: the floor modeled a 50k H/s worker, while real crypto.subtle workers measure ~400k H/s. The honest were flagged as bots; the bots could wait. We recalibrated the floor to a low quantile of honest solve time (ADR-0008) and rewrote the threat model to say plainly what a wall-clock floor can never do.

Round 1 also closed a 2⁶⁴-shaped hole: the rate limiter keyed IPv6 at /128, so one /64 block minted effectively unlimited identities — a proof-of-concept drove 5,000 requests from a single block with zero hits on the limiter (ADR-0007). And a documented invariant — tokens never outlive their challenge — turned out to be enforced nowhere; a 1-hour token was minted from a 5-second challenge. Now it's a clamp.

Round 2: the paths nobody tests

The invalid paths were the soft ones. Three hundred bad site keys, three hundred broken JSONs — served at 225 req/s with zero rate limiting, because the limiter only ran after validation. Garbage now drains the attacker's own budget. A slow-reading client could hold its connection forever — no read timeout existed; one did after that. A negative MYTHIC_BASE_DIFFICULTY produced challenges any nonce satisfied, zero-work tokens by operator foot-gun; configuration is now validated at startup and nonsense refuses to serve. Trailing JSON data was silently accepted; it's rejected. And two processes sharing one keystore could silently split-brain their signing keys — the keystore now refuses to clobber a file that changed underneath it.

Round 5: we attacked what we had just built

The sharpest finding came from the newest code — ours. The sliding-window counter stored one entry per hit, and rate-limited requests still counted. A million hits from a single identity: +16.4 MB of heap, and 595 seconds of CPU — the prune scanned the whole history on every request. A quadratic self-DoS, triggered by the cheapest attack there is: flooding and eating 429s.

Counters now cap their stored history at the limit and report saturation. Re-running the exact same flood against the fix: +0.00 MB, 714 milliseconds. O(1) per hit. The same round found that hostile tuning leaked a risk score of 500 into signed tokens (the contract says 0–100; it clamps now), and proved something we had overclaimed: with a shared Redis store, tokens from one node still don't verify against another node's JWKS, because signing keys are per-node. The docs now say so instead of pretending multi-node is solved.

What Mythic actually is now

Shipped: adaptive SHA-256 PoW with quantile-calibrated floors; the deterministic risk engine; Ed25519 decision tokens with zero-downtime key rotation (multi-key JWKS, ADR-0006); a Redis store for multi-node with atomic single-use challenges and sliding-window counters (ADR-0009), fail-closed at the HTTP edge — a store outage surfaces as 503, never as a silently-issued token; the JS SDK; a contract test suite that pins both store backends to identical semantics.

Honestly not solved: a patient attacker who solves fast and delays verification (needs the behavioral layer, M2); distributed attacks with fresh identities (needs network fingerprints, M1); cross-node token verification (needs a shared keystore, M5). The threat model lists every one — in writing, before anyone has to ask.

Why we do it this way

We said the bypass reports are the most valuable contributions this project can receive. That has to start with our own. Five rounds, twelve findings, three ADRs, every fix pinned by a regression test, every re-attack re-run against the fix — the lab eats its own dogfood first.

— XEY LABS, RED TEAM DAY
